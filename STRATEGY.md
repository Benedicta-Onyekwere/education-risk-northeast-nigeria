# Strategy Section
## Mapping Education Risk in Northeastern Nigeria: A Conflict-Sensitive Planning Tool for EBI
### Strategic Education Risk Assessment: Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa States (2024)

### Context
Northeastern Nigeria has experienced over fifteen years of sustained armed conflict driven by the Boko Haram insurgency and its offshoots. Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa states, the three states at the epicentre of this crisis, have recorded some of the highest rates of school destruction, teacher displacement, and education disruption anywhere in Sub-Saharan Africa. Millions of children in this region have had their education interrupted, not once, but repeatedly, as conflict dynamics shift across local government areas (LGAs).

EBI’s field teams operating in this context possess deep knowledge of the schools they support and the communities around them. That knowledge is invaluable. But as the RFP acknowledges, when conflict escalates or a new crisis erupts, assembling a clear picture of how education infrastructure is affected across the region is difficult to do quickly. Decisions about where to prioritise rehabilitation, where to pre-position learning materials, and where children are most at risk of losing access to education are currently made on the basis of information that arrives unevenly and is difficult to compare across areas.

What is missing is not more field knowledge. EBI’s teams already have that. What is missing is a tool that makes that knowledge visible at scale, combining it with open data sources to produce a clear, shareable picture of where the greatest concentrations of at-risk children are located relative to active conflict.

EBI’s own preliminary mapping exercise revealed significant discrepancies between school locations and child population concentrations. That finding suggests that open data, combined with the right analytical tools, can meaningfully strengthen how the organisation plans and prioritises. This proposal builds directly on that finding.

### Objectives
This project has three objectives:
1. **Enable faster, more defensible prioritisation decisions.** EBI programme officers will be able to identify, within hours rather than weeks, which districts face the highest concentration of children at risk relative to active conflict, giving headquarters and field teams a shared, evidence-based basis for deciding where to intervene first.
2. **Give EBI a planning tool they can update and own independently.** Rather than commissioning new analysis each time conflict dynamics shift, EBI staff will be able to refresh the risk picture using open data sources and interpret changes in district rankings without relying on external technical support.
3. **Build a replicable capability across EBI’s country portfolio.** EBI will be able to apply the same methodology to any of its 12 country contexts using equivalent open datasets, without starting from scratch, turning a one-country pilot into an organisational planning standard.

### Approach
The core argument of this proposal is straightforward: mapping conflict events against school locations and population distribution in northeastern Nigeria will reveal where large concentrations of school-age children are left without safe access to education. This is information EBI’s field teams know in fragments but cannot currently see all at once, across the full three-state area, in a form that can be shared with headquarters or used to justify prioritisation decisions to donors.

The proposed methodology builds on EBI’s initial experience with spatial education analysis and extends it into a conflict-sensitive planning tool suited to the organisation’s operating context. Three open, publicly available datasets will form the foundation:

*   **Conflict events: UCDP (Uppsala Conflict Data Program).** The project will use UCDP conflict data because it offers accessible, event-level coverage that EBI can reuse without licensing or registration barriers, making the tool easier for the organisation to maintain over time. Events will be filtered to Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa over a 24-month window, long enough to capture recurring conflict patterns and seasonal cycles, while recent enough to reflect current dynamics rather than the peak insurgency years of 2014 to 2016. The dataset covers state-based violence, non-state conflict, and one-sided attacks directly targeting civilians, the last category being the most relevant for education access disruption.
*   **School locations: HOT-OSM via HDX.** School location data will be sourced from the GRID3 Nigeria Education Facilities dataset, which provides the most complete ground-truthed school coverage for Nigeria including school types and operational status relevant to rehabilitation planning. Records will undergo rigorous manual cleaning to retain only verified, distinct education facilities with confirmed coordinates. The LGA will be used as the primary unit of analysis because it is the primary planning unit for both EBI and the Nigerian government.
*   **Population data: UNFPA Nigeria Subnational Population Statistics (HDX).** The project will use UNFPA subnational population data because it is already aggregated at LGA level, allowing EBI staff to update and interpret the analysis more easily without specialist geospatial processing. The 5 to 14 age band will be used as it provides granular estimates of where school-age children actually live rather than relying on outdated census totals. This will be combined with conflict and school data to produce vulnerability-weighted risk scores.

These three datasets will be combined to produce a composite risk score for each LGA. The initial weighting formula (35% conflict event frequency, 45% fatality severity, and 20% population vulnerability) is intended as a starting point rather than a fixed model. During Activity 1, EBI field staff will review and refine the balance between these components so that the final methodology reflects operational realities in the field rather than analytical assumptions made in isolation. This ensures the tool complements EBI staff expertise instead of imposing an externally defined analytical logic.

In Borno State, the methodology is designed to surface two distinct planning challenges side by side: districts where conflict is catastrophically lethal and immediate emergency response is needed, and districts where large numbers of children are exposed to sustained lower-intensity violence with insufficient schools to serve them. That distinction, invisible in conflict data alone, is exactly the kind of planning intelligence that helps EBI decide not just where the problem is, but what kind of response it requires.

As with any open-data methodology, data limitations exist and are acknowledged. School location data from HOT-OSM may be incomplete in remote and rural LGAs where humanitarian mapping coverage is thinner. UCDP conflict records may underreport incidents in areas with limited media access or restricted humanitarian presence. Where data gaps are identified during field validation, EBI staff will flag affected districts for supplementary ground-truthing rather than relying solely on the composite score. The methodology is designed to complement field knowledge precisely because data alone cannot account for everything field teams observe.

An initial prototype illustrates the feasibility of the approach, but the funded work will centre on validating the methodology with EBI staff, adapting the dashboard for operational use, and embedding it within EBI’s planning processes. The prototype is available at https://benedicta-onyekwere.github.io/education-risk-northeast-nigeria/ as an illustration of the capability to be delivered. The Nigeria case is included in this proposal as a demonstration of what the methodology produces, not as the end product itself.

The interactive map will translate this analysis into a visual that field staff and headquarters can use together. Green circles will represent districts with no recorded conflict events, relatively stable areas that may be hosting displaced populations. Orange and red circles will mark the conflict corridor where EBI’s resources should be concentrated. The map will not replace field knowledge. It will give that knowledge a geographic frame that makes it actionable at scale.

### Proposed Activities

**Activity 1: Methodology validation with EBI field staff.**
Under this project, EBI field teams in Borno, Yobe, and Adamawa will lead a structured validation of the risk rankings against their ground-level knowledge before the dashboard is deployed for operational use. Field staff will confirm whether the top-ranked districts align with their operational experience and flag any districts where the composite score does not reflect the reality they observe on the ground. This validation step is essential: the data does not replace field expertise, it is tested against it. Where discrepancies exist, the technical team will adjust the weighting formula or investigate data gaps. The result will be a risk ranking that EBI staff trust because they helped shape it. AI tools will support this process by rapidly simulating how alternative weightings change the district rankings, allowing field staff to stress-test the methodology in real time during validation sessions without requiring technical knowledge.

**Activity 2: Country adaptation and readiness assessment.**
EBI headquarters and regional leads will select the first full implementation country beyond the northeastern Nigeria pilot through a formal data readiness assessment conducted across EBI’s remaining 11 country programmes. The assessment will produce three concrete outputs: a ranked shortlist of candidate countries by data availability and operational priority, a dataset audit for each shortlisted country documenting which open sources exist and where gaps require field data collection, and an implementation recommendation including proposed adaptation strategies for the composite risk formula where local conflict dynamics differ from the Nigerian context. Based on a preliminary audit, two candidate countries are proposed for consideration: South Sudan, where UCDP, HOT-OSM, and OCHA datasets have strong coverage of conflict-affected education infrastructure, and the Democratic Republic of Congo, where EBI already operates and where HDX hosts verified school location data for the conflict-affected eastern provinces. EBI regional leads will make the final selection based on their operational priorities and the assessment findings.

**Activity 3: Capacity transfer and institutional handover.**
EBI programme staff will receive a structured capacity transfer that goes beyond a one-time handover. Staff will first apply the dashboard to a live planning decision, for example selecting districts for a forthcoming school rehabilitation assessment, so the tool is tested against a real operational need before the technical partner steps back. Training will focus on sustainability: staff will learn how to update the conflict data layer when UCDP releases new monthly records, how to add newly verified school locations to the dataset, and how to interpret changes in district risk scores over time. A supported period of independent use will follow, during which EBI staff operate the tool without technical partner involvement but with access to remote troubleshooting. The dashboard will be transferred to EBI’s own GitHub repository so it is institutionally owned and maintained. AI tools will support non-technical staff in generating district-level briefing narratives from the risk scores, reducing the time needed to translate analytical findings into donor-facing documents.

**Activity 4: Replication in a new country context.**
Using the documented methodology and the replication guide in the project repository, EBI’s staff will apply the same analytical pipeline to the agreed implementation country. EBI field teams in the new country will validate the resulting risk rankings against their ground-level knowledge, repeating the process established in Activity 1. The technical partner will provide remote support during the first replication cycle. AI tools will accelerate the data preparation phase, generating cleaning scripts, validating coordinates, and flagging anomalies, while EBI staff will lead the interpretation and contextualisation of results. By the end of this activity EBI will have independently produced a risk map for a second country, demonstrating that the methodology is a transferable organisational capability rather than a one-off technical deliverable.

### Use of Technology and AI
To deliver a tool that is both technically robust and accessible to EBI’s non-technical field staff, this project will employ an AI-augmented development workflow in which AI handles data processing tasks while human judgement governs all analytical and design decisions.

*   **AI as a data engineering assistant.** Large Language Models will be used to generate and debug Python and Pandas scripts for filtering UCDP conflict events and joining datasets by LGA boundary. AI will also generate validation functions to flag school coordinates outside Nigeria’s administrative boundaries, catching data integrity issues before analysis begins. All code will be documented and stored in the repository.
*   **Systematic data quality.** Every school record will be manually reviewed in Google Sheets, with consistent documented logic applied to each deletion decision. The cleaned dataset will be validated against the source file to confirm provenance before integration. The UNFPA population file will be downloaded directly from HDX and verified before use, ensuring all figures are traceable to a citable primary source in the repository.
*   **Human-led reasoning in analytical design.** The three components of the composite risk score and their weightings reflect deliberate choices grounded in humanitarian prioritisation principles. AI will be used to stress-test this logic by simulating how shifting weights between conflict frequency, fatality severity, and population vulnerability changes the LGA ranking. This will make the methodology transparent and adjustable without becoming a black box.
*   **Rapid prototyping and deployment.** The visual interface will be prototyped using v0 (Vercel’s AI-assisted design tool) to focus on human-centred design, ensuring the most critical risk information is visible at a glance. The final artifact will be built in React with Leaflet.js and hosted on GitHub Pages. The data layer will be separated from the visualisation layer so applying the tool to a different country requires only swapping input datasets.

### Deliverables
1. **A validated and operational dashboard for EBI** that displays conflict intensity, verified school locations, and district-level risk scores in a format usable by both field teams and headquarters. The northeastern Nigeria prototype provides the starting point, but the funded deliverable will be a field-validated tool ready for institutional use and full handover to EBI’s own repository.
2. **A cleaned and fully documented project dataset** for the implementation country, including processed conflict event data, verified school location records, population figures, and district-level risk scores, accompanied by a data dictionary and source documentation so EBI staff can maintain and update the dataset independently. The northeastern Nigeria datasets are available in the project GitHub repository at https://github.com/Benedicta-Onyekwere/education-risk-northeast-nigeria as an illustration of the standard to be delivered.
3. **A methodology note and replication guide** documenting all data sourcing decisions, cleaning and exclusion choices, how the composite risk score was calculated and how its weightings can be adjusted, and the step-by-step process for replicating the full analysis in a different country context using equivalent open data sources.

This project is deliberately focused on one practical capability that EBI can use across its country portfolio: identifying where children are most at risk of losing access to education because of conflict and limited school availability. The proposed tool will help EBI staff prioritise interventions, sequence assessments and rehabilitation efforts, and present a clearer evidence base to donors and partners. This is the capability described in EBI’s RFP, and the proposal is designed to deliver it in a form that EBI can validate, own, and replicate.
